the universe | stars

You will discover the stars

Stellar nursery: every star is born in a huge cloud of gas and dust, mostly hydrogen. Gravity pulls denser regions of the cloud together, and each clump collapses and heats up to form a protostar. The mass a star is born with decides its whole life: its brightness, its lifespan, and how it dies.

Path 1, the most massive stars: a protostar becomes a blue supergiant, an enormous, extremely hot and bright star that burns through its fuel in only a few million years. It then ends its life in a violent supernova explosion that scatters its material through space.

Path 2, very massive stars: the first stages are the same, with a protostar becoming a blue supergiant. In this path, the core collapses so completely that the star forms a black hole, an object with gravity so strong that nothing, not even light, can escape from it.

Path 3, massive stars: a protostar becomes a blue supergiant, then explodes as a Type II supernova, which happens when the core of a massive star runs out of fuel and collapses. The core that remains is crushed into a black hole.

Path 4, slightly less massive stars: the star passes through the blue supergiant stage, swells into a red giant, and can later shrink into a hotter blue giant. It then explodes as a Type II supernova, leaving behind a neutron star, an extremely dense object only about 20 km wide but heavier than the Sun.

Path 5, Sun-like stars: a protostar becomes a stable, Sun-like star that shines for about 10 billion years. When its hydrogen runs low, it swells into a red giant and then sheds its outer layers as a colorful planetary nebula. The hot core left behind is a white dwarf, which slowly cools over time. If a white dwarf has a close companion star and pulls in enough of its material, it can explode as a Type Ia supernova.

Path 6, red dwarfs: these small, cool, dim stars burn their fuel extremely slowly and can live for trillions of years, far longer than the current age of the universe. They are the most common type of star. At the very end of their lives, they slowly fade into white dwarfs.

Path 7, brown dwarfs: these objects are too small to ignite hydrogen fusion in their cores, so they are sometimes called failed stars. They glow faintly from leftover heat and slowly cool down and fade over billions of years without changing into another type of object.

Supershell and recycling: supernova explosions blow giant bubbles of hot gas called supershells into space. The elements forged inside stars, such as carbon, oxygen, and iron, are spread through these shells and enrich the gas clouds around them. New stellar nurseries form from this material, and the cycle begins again, which is how the atoms in our planet and our bodies were made.
